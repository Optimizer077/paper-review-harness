# Medical-paper inspection: historical sources through 2022

Accessed 2026-10-30. The medical shard contains **50 abstracts**, exactly **10 per year in 2018–2022**, from six exact flagship journals: New England Journal of Medicine, The Lancet, JAMA, BMJ, Nature Medicine, and Nature. Publication year and first-publication date both meet the 2022 cutoff. These are selected prominent venues, not a comprehensive or objective ranking of all medical venues.

The collector downloaded **38 openly accessible article XML bodies**. It attempted the available public links for the other **12** and recorded their failures; it did not bypass access restrictions. A downloaded body is not a completed reading. **Six abstracts and selected section excerpts from five of those papers were actually inspected** below. No article was read in full, and no supplement or underlying data was reviewed. The corpus-level `semantic_review_performed` fields intentionally remain false; this file and its JSON companion delimit the separate inspected subset.

The 1,900-character limits refer to whitespace-normalized text obtained with ElementTree `itertext()`; the 3,000-character NEJM prefixes were read before whitespace normalization. Both include any headings or inline table/caption material in the selected section. Only those prefixes were inspected; a locator is not a claim that an entire section was reviewed. The JSON companion records the exact extraction rules and inspected-prefix hashes.

## Transferable checks for the writing skill

1. **Fix the study question before polishing.** Record population or biological system, design, intervention/exposure, comparator, endpoint, setting, and time window. Match the title, abstract objective, methods, results, and final claim to that record. The amoxicillin and NITRIC papers provide concrete contrasting examples of noninferiority and superiority objectives.
2. **Lock the unit attached to every number.** Store screened records, reports, studies, participants, clusters, clinicians, encounters, doses, and independent experimental units separately. Check denominators when a manuscript moves from enrollment to available-case analysis. The antipsychotic review and EMBED trial show why matching a bare numeral is insufficient.
3. **Keep outcome hierarchy intact.** A positive secondary endpoint, adoption measure, biomarker, or animal response cannot replace a neutral primary endpoint or imply a patient benefit that was not measured. Use EMBED and the cGAS-STING study to test this boundary in different research genres.
4. **Preserve the comparison and scale.** A risk ratio, odds ratio, absolute risk difference, standardized mean difference, credible interval, and confidence interval are not interchangeable. Keep units, analysis population, interval type, and follow-up beside the result. Do not silently recompute missing intervals or clinical importance from a p-value.
5. **Make qualifications travel with the claim.** Restrict an inference to the studied population, setting, tissue, model, intervention timing, and analysis. Keep residual confounding and sensitivity-analysis disagreement visible in the abstract and discussion, as illustrated by the Scotland vaccine study.
6. **Turn jargon checks into meaning checks.** Maintain a terminology registry that maps an abbreviation to a definition, population/system, endpoint role, and first-use location. Necessary technical terms can repeat. Flag a synonym only when it changes meaning, hides an endpoint change, or confuses the unit of analysis; do not rewrite all repeated terms for variety.
7. **Explain a useful gap, not an empty novelty slogan.** The inspected introductions move from a clinical or biological decision to what the available evidence leaves uncertain. A claim that “no one has studied X” still needs a verified search and an explanation of why X matters.
8. **Score inspectable evidence, with explicit unknowns.** Evidence alignment, design completeness, terminology stability, uncertainty preservation, and readability need separate judgments. Lack of supplied evidence should produce “unassessed” or a named gap, not an invented numerical rating. Automated counts can check IDs, literal values, or word length; they cannot certify causal validity or scientific quality.

These are editorial observations from a small, deliberately sampled subset. They are not evidence that a writing pattern caused acceptance, that all 50 papers share the pattern, or that one structure fits all journals. Apply a study-design reporting checklist and current target-venue instructions separately when preparing an actual submission.

## Reproduce the collection

```powershell
python paper-writing/scripts/collect_historical_medical.py --output-root research/historical-2022
```

The script requires Python and `requests`, queries the public Europe PMC core API, uses at most four workers, a 25-second request timeout, and at most one retry for transient failures. It writes `shards/medical.jsonl`, `shards/medical-log.jsonl`, and `shards/medical-summary.json`; public article bodies go to `fulltexts/med-*.xml`. The log records source queries, selection counts, rejection reasons, and every public full-text attempt.

Selection is a **journal/year-stratified convenience sample**: two papers per year from NEJM, Lancet, JAMA, and BMJ; one each from Nature Medicine and Nature. It screens the first 100 core results per stratum, favors open full-text availability, and then sorts by PMID. Nature is additionally restricted to health-related title/abstract terms. Excluded metadata types include letters, editorials, corrections, protocols, preprints, retractions, and narrative reviews. These choices create coverage and access biases and do not constitute a systematic review.
